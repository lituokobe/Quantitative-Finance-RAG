# What Is a Principal-Protected Note (PPN)? Benefits and Risks Explained

## What Does Principal-Protected Note Mean?

A principal-protected note (PPN) guarantees that investors will, at the least, recover their initial investment when the note reaches maturity. Even if the underlying assets perform poorly, the principal remains secure. PPNs suit risk-averse investors who wish to participate in potential market gains without losing their principal. However, the security of a PPN is contingent on the issuer's creditworthiness, and investors should be prepared for long-term commitments.

### Key Takeaways

- Principal-protected notes (PPNs) are fixed-income securities that ensure the return of an investor's principal, regardless of the underlying asset's performance.
- These notes combine a zero-coupon bond with an option linked to an asset, index, or benchmark, providing potential gains from favorable market movements if held to maturity.
- The guarantee of principal depends on the creditworthiness of the issuer, making the security unsecured and subject to issuer risk.
- PPNs require investors to hold them until maturity for full payout; early withdrawals may result in penalties and reduced principal.
- Past incidents, such as the Lehman Brothers collapse, highlight the complexity and critical scrutiny required when investing in PPNs.

## How Principal-Protected Notes Work

A principal protected note (PPN) is a structured finance product that guarantees a rate of return of at least the principal amount invested, as long as the note is held to maturity. A PPN is structured as a zero-coupon bond – a bond that makes no interest payment until it matures – and an option with a payoff that is linked to an underlying asset, index, or benchmark. Based on the performance of the linked asset, index or benchmark, the payoff will vary. For example, if the payoff is linked to an equity index, such as Russell 2000, and the index rises 30%, the investor will receive the full 30% gain. In effect, the principal protected securities promise to return an investor’s principal, at the time of maturity, with the added gain from the index’s performance if that index trades within a certain range.

One downside is that the guarantee relies on the issuer's or guarantor's creditworthiness. If the issuer goes bankrupt and can't pay, investors could lose their principal, negating the guarantee. Because these notes are unsecured debt, investors have lower priority than secured creditors.

Furthermore, investors must hold these notes until maturity in order to receive the full payout. These notes often have long maturities, so investors may tie up funds for years and pay annual imputed interest. Early withdrawals might incur fees, and partial withdrawals can reduce the final payout.

## Risks and Limitations of Principal-Protected Notes

The risks of principal-protected notes became clear after Lehman Brothers collapsed during the 2008 credit crisis. Lehman brothers had issued many of these notes and brokers were pushing it in the portfolios of their clients who had little to no knowledge of these products. PPN returns were more complex than they seemed to clients. For an investor to earn index returns and get their principal back, terms may require the index not to drop 25% or more from its starting point. It also can't rise more than 27%. If the index exceeds those levels during the holding period, the investors receive only their principal back.

Investors who avoid complex PPNs may choose principal-protected funds instead. Principal protected funds are money managed funds that consist mostly of principal protected notes structured to protect an investor’s principal. The returns on these funds are taxed as ordinary income rather than capital gains or tax-advantaged dividends. Furthermore, fees that are charged by the fund are used to fund the derivative positions used to guarantee the principal returns and minimize risk.

## The Bottom Line

Principal-protected notes (PPNs) offer risk-averse investors a way to safeguard their initial investment while still participating in potential market gains. However, one must remember that these notes are only as secure as the issuer's creditworthiness. Investors may face the risk of losing their principal if the issuer defaults. Additionally, the requirement to hold these notes until maturity could result in limited liquidity and potential costs. Those who prefer not to deal with individual PPN complications might consider principal-protected funds. Understanding the limitations and conditions, such as restrictions on index performance, is crucial when considering PPNs as an investment option.
